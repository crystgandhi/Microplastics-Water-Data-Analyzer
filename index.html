Case studies on capturing and processing microplastics in water and wastewater.

Ceramic microfiltration for industrial laundry wastewater

Study scale
Eight-week pilot using industrial laundry wastewater
Reported result
More than 97% removal of microplastic fibers. The study reported an influent concentration of 38,616 ± 1,140 fibers per liter.
What happens after capture?
Filtration separates fibers from the water into a concentrated residue that requires disposal or further processing.

Magnetic ferrofluid capture · Mia Heller

Study scale
Student prototype presented at the 2025 Regeneron International Science and Engineering Fair.
How it works
An oil-based liquid containing tiny magnetic particles is mixed into the water. Microplastics associate with the oil. A magnetic separator and a layered filter then separate the captured plastic and recover ferrofluid for reuse.
Microplastic removal
95.52% under the prototype’s test conditions.
Ferrofluid recovery
87.15% of the ferrofluid used was recovered.
What happens after capture?
The system separates the captured plastic from the water and recirculates the recovered ferrofluid.

Original ISEF project abstract

Ultrasonic microplastic filtration · Justin Huang and Victoria Ou

Study scale
Student laboratory prototypes presented at Regeneron ISEF in 2024 and 2025.
How it works
Piezoelectric transducers generate ultrasound in a tube. The acoustic force blocks suspended particles as water flows through.
2024 laboratory tests
Polyethylene removal was 84–94% with one stage and 94–96% with two stages at flow rates of 10–40 mL/min.
2025 follow-up
The students tested polyethylene and polystyrene particles in the 20–200 µm size range. In polyethylene tests spanning 40–300 mL/min, one two-stage configuration reached up to 96.5% removal.
What happens after capture?
The 2025 prototype included an automated particle retrieval and cleaning system.

U.S. comparison context

Current records do not support a defensible ranking of U.S. states by microplastic pollution. Studies differ in sample type, particle-size cutoffs, collection dates, and analytical methods, and nationwide coverage is incomplete.

Alabama River · particle count

A 2025 study reports 12–135 microplastic particles per liter across sampled Alabama River sites and seasons. This is a result from one study, not evidence that Alabama has the highest pollution in the United States.

Read the 2025 study · PubMed record

Galveston Bay · polymer mass

A 2026 Gulf estuary study reports 0.82–19.8 micrograms per liter, with higher mass concentrations at the Houston Ship Channel. These are mass-based results and cannot be ranked directly against particle counts per liter or the existing Texas record reported in particles per cubic meter.

Read the 2026 study

Chesapeake watershed · presence and particle types

The 2026 report describes plastic particles at all 15 sampled sites and reports particle types, but provides no concentration values. It supports a presence finding for those sampled sites, not a quantitative pollution ranking.

Read the original report (PDF)

Chicago-area waterways · fish, water, and sediment

Hou et al. (2021) examined museum fish specimens collected from 1900–2018 and sampled fish, water, and sediment at Hickory Creek, Brewster Creek/Fox River, and Lake Michigan at Calumet Park in 2018. Fish microplastics increased significantly after 1950; particles detected in fish digestive tracts were fibers. In 2018, sediment results were 830 ± 150 particles/L at Hickory Creek, 330 ± 170 particles/L at Brewster, and 307 ± 56 particles/L at Calumet. The study found no significant difference in water-column concentrations among the three locations. Keep fish, water, and sediment as separate sample matrices; sediment values are not water concentrations and should not be ranked against water records.

Hou et al. (2021), Ecological Applications · Associated Mendeley Data

Methods & policy sources

These resources add measurement-method guidance, federal policy context, and local program information. They do not all provide river concentration records. Only enter measured values in Browse records when a source reports a defined sample, method, location, result, and unit.

Raman methods for microplastic analysis · HORIBA

HORIBA describes a laboratory workflow covering sample preparation, filtration, particle imaging, Raman analysis, and polymer identification. Use this manufacturer resource to explain why analytical method, filter properties, and size cutoff matter when interpreting records. It does not provide U.S. river measurements; instrument claims are vendor information.

HORIBA: Raman microplastic analysis · HORIBA: Microplastics analysis workflow

Water and wastewater treatment overview · Hazen & Sawyer

This engineering perspective discusses analytical size limits, treatment of detectable particles, and possible transfer of captured microplastics into sludge and biosolids. It also raises questions about treatment materials and particle fragmentation. Treat these as technical context and reported concerns, not as site-specific measurements or settled conclusions. The article provides no river concentration dataset.

Federal drinking-water policy · CCL 6 and UCMR 6

EPA’s draft Sixth Contaminant Candidate List (CCL 6) includes microplastics among groups prioritized for research and possible future regulatory consideration. Reuters reported the early-April announcement as a move to monitor drinking-water impacts. The later EPA UCMR 6 proposal, issued July 1, 2026, proposed monitoring 30 chemical contaminants during 2028–2030 and did not include microplastics. These are different steps: draft CCL 6 inclusion does not itself require water utilities to test. As of November 25, 2026, EPA’s UCMR 6 page still describes the monitoring rule as proposed. STOMP concerns measurement, health effects, and potential removal from the human body, not river monitoring.

Great Lakes tributaries · USGS interactive visualization

The USGS visualization communicates results from 29 tributaries sampled during 2014–2015. Fibers made up an average 71% of particles. The study found urban-watershed associations for fragments, films, and foams; fibers did not show the same association. The visualization is useful for exploring the findings, while the older sampling dates should remain visible.

Open USGS microplastics visualization · USGS 2014–2015 data release · Baldwin et al. (2016) study

Philadelphia waterways · local monitoring and response

Philadelphia Water Department reports collecting and characterizing microplastics in city rivers and streams since 2017, with future work planned on amounts, spatial and seasonal patterns, and particle types. The page also reports that in FY 2025 the department removed nearly 35 tons of trash, recyclables, and debris, including about 4.6 tons of plastics. Those cleanup totals describe larger collected material, not measured microplastic concentrations.

This educational overview discusses how plastic litter breaks into microplastics and describes possible pathways through stormwater, wastewater, and sewage sludge. It was updated June 2, 2025. It is background reading, not a Pennsylvania water-sampling dataset. Its bottled-water and tap-water figures cite a 2018 study, so they are not presented as current local measurements in this app.

ITRC · regulatory context

The Interstate Technology & Regulatory Council summarizes regulatory programs and includes a state survey conducted in summer 2021. Its survey findings are historical and should not be read as a current state-by-state status; ITRC notes that regulations may have changed and its Appendix C tables are periodically updated. This is regulatory context, not a source of water-concentration measurements. Check the linked table and underlying agency sources before describing current requirements.

Measurlabs · single-sample comparison example

This 2023 laboratory blog compares one sample of each of five water types collected in Finland, including Vantaa River water. It reports 12 particles/L in that single river sample (4 polystyrene and 8 polypropylene particles) using Raman spectroscopy, with most measured particles across sample types in the 1–50 µm range. The authors explicitly caution that one sample per type is insufficient for broad conclusions. Use this as an example of why sample size, location, particle-size range, and polymer identification matter; it is not a U.S. river record or a representative river concentration.

American Scientist · Great Lakes and drinking-water overview

Sherri A. Mason’s 2019 overview summarizes Great Lakes, wastewater, tap-water, and bottled-water research. It reports that the cited bottled-water analysis counted particles down to 6.5 µm, while the tap-water analysis used a 100 µm cutoff. The article reports 325 particles/L for bottled water and 5.5 particles/L for tap water, but those totals cover different particle-size ranges and should not be treated as a like-for-like concentration comparison. This is a research overview, not a standalone dataset; use its cited primary papers for study-level records. The app’s Great Lakes tributary source card links the relevant USGS data and Baldwin et al. study.

Penn Water Center · bottled-water measurement context

This commentary raises the issue that bottled-water microplastic counts vary depending on which source and measurement results are consulted. Use it as context for explaining why reported counts need their sampling and analytical methods alongside them. It is not a U.S. river dataset; use primary studies, rather than this commentary, for quantitative records.

Sierra Nevada snowfall study

Karapetrova et al. (2026) used FTIR spectroscopy to identify microplastics in fresh snowfall. Coauthor Win Cowger lists the Moore Institute for Plastic Pollution Research and UC Riverside as affiliations.

Reported average concentrations in melted fresh snowfall ranged from 11.8 to 37.5 particles/L at remote sites and from 68.3 to 92.9 particles/L at the site affected by human activity. Compare these snowfall measurements with other snowfall studies using compatible methods and particle size ranges.
