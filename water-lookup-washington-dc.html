Why particle properties matter

When comparing concentrations, check the polymer type, particle size range, and surface condition (such as weathering) when the study reports them. Two samples with the same particle count per liter can contain different kinds of particles. Different minimum particle sizes can also affect which particles each study counts.

In laboratory tests, Qian and colleagues found that tested microplastics generated reactive oxygen species at their surfaces in water. More water-repellent surfaces showed greater activity under the study conditions. River particle counts alone cannot tell us how reactive those particles are.

Qian et al. (2026), Nature · Microplastic–water interfaces

Can I compare these records?

Check these details in each study before comparing concentrations:

  • Sample type and reporting basis. Compare like samples, such as surface water with surface water. Particles per liter measures count, while micrograms per liter measures mass. Compare results on the same basis after converting compatible units.
  • Particle size range. Check both limits, especially the smallest particle counted. A study that counts particles down to 50 µm can include particles that a study starting at 300 µm misses. Compare a shared size range if both studies report results for it.
  • Analytical method. Nile Red staining screens for suspected plastic particles. FTIR can identify the polymer of individual particles analyzed. Pyrolysis GC–MS measures polymer mass in the sample and does not provide individual particle counts or sizes.
  • Contamination controls. Look for field or laboratory blanks and check whether the authors adjusted counts using them. Treat any missing detail as unknown.

Chesapeake tributaries: urban areas and rainfall

A NOAA-supported analysis of Chesapeake surface-water samples found higher microplastic concentrations near urban areas and after heavy rain. This points to urban runoff and rainfall-related transport as plausible pathways, but it did not identify a single source for each river.

Chesapeake Bay photo essay: what researchers found

In 2011, 59 of 60 samples from the Patapsco, Magothy, Rhode and Corsica rivers contained microplastics. The photo essay shows examples including fragments, fibers, pellets, sheets and polystyrene. Roads and storm drains, wastewater effluent and shipping were discussed as possible pathways; the photographs do not establish the source of individual particles. These are historical study results.

Tidal Potomac: several sources tested, none dominant statistically

A 2025 preliminary study sampled urban, suburban, agricultural, natural/wetland, stormwater-outfall, and wastewater-outfall sites during baseflow and stormflow. It found no statistically significant difference among land-use groups. Baseflow counts were higher at almost all sites, so stormflow did not produce a uniform increase.

Read the 2025 Potomac source-tracking report

Interpretation: urban runoff, wastewater, and litter are plausible routes into Maryland waterways, but current local studies do not establish one statewide or river-specific “main source.” The Potomac report is preliminary; its land-use groups were not statistically different.

Research: transport and sediment storage

Christensen and colleagues (2020) examined 14 sediment samples at six sites along three Virginia waterways. Microplastics in floodplains were as abundant as, or more abundant than, those in channel sediments; particles were also found aggregated with natural sediment. This study concerns sediment and floodplain transport, not water concentration in particles/L. River-specific values have not yet been verified in this app.

North Carolina studies: particle types and possible pathways

These summaries describe study areas and sample types; they are not measurements for every river or proof of individual pollution sources.

Neuse River Basin · water and sediment

For particles larger than 335 μm in water, polyethylene, polypropylene and polystyrene were common in FTIR analysis. PET was common among smaller particles examined by Raman spectroscopy. Runoff, wastewater and degraded litter are possible inputs; the study does not identify a source for each particle.

Blue Ridge headwater streams · water

Fibers comprised about 90% of suspected particles in the Richland Creek and Cullasaja tributary samples. The study also examined polymer composition and atmospheric deposition; atmospheric inputs and development were investigated as possible contributors.

Miller et al. (2024): read study
Catawba River Basin · water

Colored fibers were frequently observed, alongside fragments. The study compared selected recreational and upstream/downstream sites. Identification was based on microscopy and physical characteristics; the polymer composition was not confirmed in the described method, and potential sources are not proven by the comparison.

Selected coastal sites · sediment

Fragments and fibers were reported in beach and estuarine sediments sampled in North Carolina and Virginia. Raman analysis of a subset identified polypropylene, PET, polystyrene, polyethylene, nylon and other materials. These results describe selected coastal sites, not a river measurement.

How the measurements are collected

The unit depends on what researchers sampled. These simple illustrations help explain the three sample types in this dataset.

Surface-water particle abundance

particles/km²

Counts particles collected across a measured water-surface area.

Water concentration

particles/L

Counts particles found in a known volume of water, often after filtering.

Sediment abundance

particles/kg or particles/g

Counts particles relative to the mass of sediment collected.

Illustrations are explanatory, not photographs of the cited sampling sites or experiments.

How microplastics reach water bodies

Plastic particles can be created directly at small sizes or form as larger plastic breaks down. Rain, runoff, wastewater, wind, and direct releases can carry them from land into streams, rivers, lakes, and estuaries.

Litter breaks into fragments

Sunlight and weathering can break bottles, bags, and other plastic debris into smaller pieces. Wind and rain can move litter and fragments toward drains and waterways.

Land surfaces→Runoff / wind

Wastewater effluent

Washing synthetic fabrics releases microfibers. Some particles can pass through treatment or be carried with treatment residuals; treated effluent can enter surface waters.

Homes→Wastewater plant→Waterway

Stormwater and road runoff

Rain can wash road dust, tire-wear particles, and plastic litter from streets and parking areas into storm drains, streams, and rivers.

Roads→Storm drains→Streams

Direct releases

Plastic manufacturing pellets or other plastic materials can spill during handling or transport and enter nearby waterways. These are potential pathways, not confirmed sources for the records shown.

Handling / spills→Nearby water
Water flows can carry particles downstream. Depending on particle properties and water conditions, they may float, remain suspended, or settle into sediment.

Important: this overview describes general pathways reported by NOAA and EPA. It does not identify which source contributed particles to a particular river or prove a source for any concentration record in this app.
